PROJECT 05 · FIELD RESEARCH

Waste, inequality
& urban reuse.

Ethnographic research in Berlin exploring waste management, social inequality and the reuse of urban spaces and materials.

Academic field research · Berlin

The research question

What impact do social inequalities have on waste management in Berlin?

Within a wider group project exploring sustainability and urban identity, I focused on waste management. My individual research examined how waste was perceived, made visible and repurposed across selected sites.

My contribution

  • Conducted field observations and gathered photographic evidence during the fieldtrip.
  • Used informal interviews, observations and academic literature to explore the research question.
  • Created a map to organise observations and examine spatial patterns.
  • Produced an individual essay and reflective commentary.
  • Contributed the methodology section of the group’s sustainability presentation.

Research approach

I compared evidence from selected locations, including Tempelhof, Kreuzberg, Museum Island and the Suicide Circus area. Triangulating observations, photographs and informal interviews helped me consider different interpretations of the same spaces.

The study organised its analysis around three themes: perceptions of waste, its visibility and its repurposing. Literature on urban metabolism and reclaimed spaces provided a framework for interpreting field evidence.

Exploratory insights

The observations suggested differences in the visibility of waste between selected spaces and raised questions about inequality, maintenance and the value attached to urban areas.

Discarded bottles illustrated how the same material could be understood as litter or as an economic resource. Community reuse projects at Tempelhof offered another perspective on how materials and spaces can gain new purposes.

Ethics & limitations

The essay records informed consent for informal interviews and for photographs of people or their possessions. Public transport and limited paper use supported a lower-impact approach to fieldwork.

Fieldwork was limited to one week and selected sites. The findings are exploratory interpretations, rather than a representative survey of Berlin or proof that inequality caused the observed patterns. Observations alone do not establish citywide cleaning budgets or resource allocation.

Reflection

The project developed my qualitative research, evidence synthesis and collaboration skills. Working alongside teammates investigating food, fashion and spaces helped connect individual findings with a wider sustainability question.

My reflection identified the value of preparing literature earlier, balancing individual and shared responsibilities, and adapting research methods through discussion and supervisor feedback.
